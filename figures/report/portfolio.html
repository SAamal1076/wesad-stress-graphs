Stress detection that still works when the schedule changes

A leakage-checked, pre-registered study of acute-stress detection from chest and wrist wearables on the open WESAD dataset (15 people). It shows how the benchmark can be won by reading the clock, and builds a model that reads the body instead.

Fifteen sessions, two timetables

Every recording follows almost the same schedule, so the time since the start already tells you which condition is running. This is the shortcut a stress model must not learn.

95.7%accuracy for stress vs non-stress from the minutes since the start alone, under the standard leave-one-person-out test: higher than the original benchmark (93.1%).

Change the protocol order and the shortcut collapses

Training on one protocol order and testing on the other separates reading the clock from reading the body.

Per-person macro-F1 for stress vs non-stress, mean of 15 people. * Scored without the first two baseline minutes.

  • 0.943*macro-F1 on a protocol order the model never saw, without any personal calibration
  • 94.0%leave-one-person-out accuracy, against 93.1% in the original WESAD paper, also leave-one-person-out
  • 4features that drift with time in session; removing them is what made the model robust to a new schedule

How the model reads the body

Simple, interpretable steps, each fitted only on the people used for training.

  1. 1

    Cut

    60 s windows inside one condition, every 5 s.

  2. 2

    Describe

    38 physiological features and a coupling graph learned between heart rate, breathing, skin conductance, muscle and movement.

  3. 3

    Remove the clock

    Drop four features that drift with time in session: skin temperature and tonic skin conductance.

  4. 4

    Classify

    Logistic regression, p = σ(w·z + b), tuned inside the training people only.

  5. 5

    Test on new people and a new schedule

    Leave-one-person-out, and train on one protocol order, test on the other.

Under stress, the heart stops following movement

A learned coupling graph between body signals shows the heart-rate↔movement link weakening under stress in 15 of 15 people, and not under amusement.

What carries over to stress estimation in VR training

The study was designed around what a VR training system needs from a stress model.

Works for a new traineeEvaluated on people never seen in training, and on a protocol order never seen in training, with drift checks that expose time-in-session shortcuts.
InterpretableA linear model on named physiological features, and body↔movement coupling as a readable, person-relative marker of stress.
Near-real-timeCausal graph learning updated every 5 s; a heart-rate index flags the start of the stressor in a median 13 s.
Physiology with behaviourCoupling between body signals and movement, built to take more behavioural channels such as head, hand and gaze in VR.
ReproducibleEvery analysis committed to git before it was run, leakage tests, 25 unit tests, one script reruns everything from the raw data.
