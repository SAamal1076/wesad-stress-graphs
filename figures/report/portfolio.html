Stress detection that still works when the schedule changes

Can a wearable recognise acute stress in a person it has never seen, without being fooled by the timetable of the experiment? On the open WESAD dataset (15 people, chest and wrist sensors), this study finds a hidden shortcut in the standard benchmark, removes it, and keeps a simple model that reads the body.

What stress looks like in the body

One typical participant's whole session. During the stress test (a public speech and mental arithmetic), heart rate and sweating rise. Skin conductance also creeps up all session long: a slow drift that quietly tracks the time.

The benchmark has a hidden shortcut

The study ran in two orders of events: 8 people did amusement before the stress test, 7 did it after. Within each order, all sessions follow almost the same timetable, so the time since the start already reveals which condition is running.

A model that reads only the clock beats the published benchmark. High scores on this dataset can therefore mean "knows the schedule" rather than "recognises stress".

A fairer test removes it

Train on people who did the conditions in one order, test on people who did them in the other order. Reading the clock no longer works; reading the body still does.

The model in five simple steps

Every step is fitted only on the people used for training, so nothing about the test person leaks in.

Under stress, the heart stops following movement

Each line between two signals shows how strongly they move together, learned with a random-walk graph method. At rest, heart rate tracks movement; during the stress test that link weakens, in every one of the 15 people, and not during amusement.

Why it matters for stress estimation in VR training

A VR training system needs a stress model that works on a new trainee, explains itself, reacts in time, and combines body and behaviour. The study was built around those needs.
